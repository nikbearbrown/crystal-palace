# Nasmyth steam hammer — dimension spike (gate document)

**Prop:** Wave 2, item 8 — `hp-working-machine-exhibit`, ONE documented
working exhibit for CP024. **Selection (Bear, 2026-10-27): Nasmyth's
steam hammer**, as exhibited by Nasmyth & Co (J. Nasmyth), Patricroft,
Manchester, at the Great Exhibition, Hyde Park, 1851.

**Plan gate (SCENE-BUILD-PLAN.md, item 8):** "Do not build a generic
'Victorian machine'. If no single exhibit can be documented, this slot
stays empty… the film must not invent a functioning engine assembly."
Leads named by the plan: Muse 0316 / 1293 (checked in §5).

## VERDICT: BUILDABLE — form from the exhibit's own photograph;
## absolute scale ASSUMED / UNCONFIRMED, flagged

- **The exhibit is identified beyond doubt.** *Official Descriptive
  and Illustrated Catalogue* (1851), **Class VI (Manufacturing
  Machines and Tools), No. 236: "NASMYTH, J., Manchester. Steam
  hammer."** — with an editorial note (§1). The Class VI jury report
  discusses it under the same number, "(236)" (§1). It received a
  **Council Medal** (one of only two Council Medals in Class VI
  machinery coverage; also Popular Science Monthly's 1897 Nasmyth
  sketch: "a council medal for his steam hammer").
- **A dedicated photograph of the exhibit itself exists** —
  Ferrier's plate for *Reports by the Juries*, RCIN 2800024 (§2) —
  and its proportions are measured in §6.
- **No dimension for the 1851 exhibit itself was found in any
  source** — not in the catalogue entry (which gives none), not in
  the jury report, not in the press/biographical coverage found.
  The morning desk note
  (`~/workspace/crystal-palace-film/nasmyth-dimension-spike-2026-10-27.md`)
  reached the same negative result independently. The catalogue
  Class VI entry was the decisive open source for this question:
  it was checked, and it yields no dimension.
- **GUARDRAIL CORRECTION (parent agent, 2026-10-27 — applied before
  any dimension was fixed):** Science Museum Group object co46218
  is **NOT a model of the 1851 exhibit**. It is a 1:4 model of the
  **FIRST hammer** (1839 design / 1842 patent). It is treated in
  this spike as **MECHANISM evidence only** (tup + piston rod
  between two standards; steam lifts, gravity drops; Wilson's 1843
  regulating device) — **NOT a dimension source**. Its record as
  read gives no model measurements to scale from, and no scale is
  taken from it. An earlier draft of this spike derived the build
  height by scaling that model ×4; that derivation is **withdrawn**
  and superseded by this correction. The IMechE 1:8 model is
  likewise not the exhibit: it is based on the Patricroft works
  machine with the 1847 self-acting improvement.
- **Absolute scale is therefore ASSUMED, not measured:** the build
  adopts a nominal overall height **H = 5.00 m** purely as a
  modelling assumption applied to the photograph's own proportions
  (§6–§7), flagged **UNCONFIRMED** with an indicative ±30%
  (3.5–6.5 m) range. Nothing is borrowed from any other Nasmyth
  hammer (§4 lists the rejected figures explicitly, with the trap
  figures now named). Absence of a dimension is never permission
  to borrow one.

## 1. The exhibit in print — Class VI, No. 236

*Official Descriptive and Illustrated Catalogue of the Great
Exhibition* (1851), Class VI, entry 236 (text as transcribed in
Grace's Guide's transcription of the printed catalogue; the entry
was cross-checked against the jury report's exhibit number and the
catalogue's own index sequence — neighbouring entries 230–242 are
all Class VI machine-tool makers):

> **236. NASMYTH, J., Manchester.** Steam hammer.
> [This steam hammer is capable of adjustment of power in a degree
> highly remarkable. While it is possible to obtain enormous
> impulsive force by its means, it can be graduated as to descend
> with power only sufficient to break an egg-shell. –R. E.]

Two cautions about this entry:
- The bracketed note is an **editorial capability claim printed in
  the catalogue**, not a report of anything performed at the
  Exhibition. See §4 on the egg: it must not be staged as an
  Exhibition moment.
- The entry gives **no dimensions, no weight, no fall** — nothing
  dimensional can be quoted from it.

The Class VI **jury report** (*Reports by the Juries*, Vol. II) —
as quoted in the British Library's catalogue record for the Ferrier
photograph (X.1067, 2(426b)):

> "The admirable steam hammer of Messrs. Nasmyth and Co. (236) is
> too well known to require any lengthened notice in this place.
> It is only to be regretted that the na…" *(BL record truncates
> the quotation here.)*

Nasmyth also appears separately at **Class V, No. 41** ("A
steam-engine" — the small direct-acting engine, also medal­led).
That is a different exhibit; the morning desk note's "Class V, not
Class VI" correction applies to *that* entry only. The hammer is
Class VI, No. 236, as the build brief stated.

**Award:** Council Medal for the steam hammer (Popular Science
Monthly, May 1897, sketch of Nasmyth; Grace's Guide's J. Nasmyth
and Co timeline: "1851 Award at the 1851 Great Exhibition… Reports
of the Juries: Class VI").

## 2. The photograph — RCIN 2800024

Claude-Marie Ferrier, "The Great Exhibition, 1851: Steam Hammer by
Nasmyth", salted-paper print from *Reports by the Juries*, Vol. II
(RCIN 2800001), **RCIN 2800024**:
https://www.rct.uk/collection/2800024/the-great-exhibition-1851-steam-hammer-by-nasmyth
(also catalogued by albert.rct.uk). RCT's description: "Photograph
of a triangular shaped machinery on two feet with a central shaft.
Inscribed 'J. Nasmyth's patent' and 'J. Nasmyth & Co Engineers,
Manchester'." Copies of the same print: British Library C.44.h.5,
2(426b) and X.1067, 2(426b) ("Close view of steam hammer on display
at the Great Exhibition"); Science Museum Group print from the
collodion negative (object 1982-399/1, print 22.9 × 17.6 cm).

The image (local copy: `refs/ferrier-1851-steam-hammer-rcin2800024.jpg`,
SSPL copy via the Science and Industry Museum) shows the machine
front-on, standing on the boarded Exhibition floor against a plain
backdrop, tup at rest just above the anvil. Everything the build
asserts about FORM comes from this photograph: two A-form standards
with concave throats, cylinder on top spanning them, bolted valve
chest on the cylinder's front, long hand lever to the left, valve
rod and linkage down the left side, handwheel low on the left,
maker's plates on the right standard ("J NASMYTH & Co / ENGINEERS /
MANCHESTER") and on the tup block ("J NASMYTH'S / PATENT"). There
is no human figure in frame, so the photograph carries **no internal
scale** — it is confirmed for **form/identity only** and cannot by
itself size the model.

## 3. The SMG 1:4 model (co46218) — MECHANISM evidence only

Science Museum Group, object **co46218** (accession **1857-3**,
the South Kensington Museum's earliest inventory series; M-register
M 24): "Model of James Nasmyth's direct-action steam hammer, scale
1:4." Maker: James Nasmyth; made 1843–1857 (catalogue); materials
cast iron, wrought iron, gunmetal, brass. Record:
https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co46218/model-of-james-nasmyths-direct-action-steam-hammer-models-representations-steam-hammers
(model photograph: `refs/smg-model-co46218.jpg`.)

**Guardrail correction — read before using this model for anything:**
1. **The model is NOT a model of the 1851 exhibit.** SMG's own
   description: "This model represents **the first steam hammer**,
   invented by James Nasmyth in 1839 and patented by him in 1842",
   fitted with Wilson's 1843 self-acting regulating device. Nothing
   in the record ties the model to the Exhibition; it entered the
   museum in 1857.
2. **Whether the exhibit WAS the first hammer is not established**
   by any source found in this spike. The model's scale therefore
   cannot transfer to the exhibit, and no attempt is made to do so.
3. **No dimension is taken from the model.** Its record as read
   gives no model measurements to scale from, and the model is
   used **only** as mechanism evidence for §8 (single-acting
   direct-action pattern; tup + piston rod guided between two
   standards; steam below the piston lifts, gravity drops; Wilson's
   1843 regulating device with small overhead cylinder, levers and
   vertical motion rod). The exhibit photograph is consistent with
   that mechanism (valve chest, hand lever, overhead gear and
   motion rod all visible).
4. The IMechE 1:8 model is likewise excluded as a dimension
   source: it is based on the Patricroft works machine with the
   1847 self-acting improvement — a later form, not the exhibit.

**Conclusion:** co46218 contributes mechanism and pattern
confirmation only. The build's absolute scale is a nominal
assumption (§7), flagged unconfirmed, and NOTES.md must say so.

## 4. Figures explicitly NOT used (borrowed-dimension traps)

- **30 cwt hammer block, 4 ft clear fall** — Nasmyth's
  *Autobiography* (ed. Smiles, 1883): "I constructed one of 30 cwt.
  of hammer block, with a clear four feet range of fall" — that is
  the **demonstration hammer built for his own Patricroft works
  after the June 1842 patent** (Smiles, *Industrial Biography*:
  "The first hammer, of 30 cwt., was made for the Patricroft
  works"). **Not** the exhibit; **not** stated as the exhibit's
  spec.
- **The next order, Rushton & Eckersley of Bolton: 5 tons, 5 ft
  fall** — also a different hammer, also excluded. (Named here so
  the trap is recognisable: any "5 ton / 5 ft" figure attached to
  the exhibit is this order, not the Exhibition machine.)
- **The egg-in-a-wineglass passage:** verdict **unresolved
  secondary-source conflict**. The Autobiography's egg passage is
  a **Devonport Dockyard demonstration** (2½-ton hammer, before the
  Lords of the Admiralty) in Ch. 15, and the egg-in-a-wineglass
  passage sits with the Patricroft hammer; a Science Museum blog
  ("If I Had A Hammer") relocates an egg demo to the 1851
  Exhibition. The sources conflict and the Exhibition staging is
  **not verified**. CP024 therefore shows **generic tup motion
  only**; the egg is not staged or claimed anywhere in the package.
  (The catalogue's own 1851 editorial note in §1 claims only the
  *capability* of graduating the blow to egg-shell force.)
- **The 4-ton pile-driving hammer** (Autobiography Ch. 15: "the
  eighty blows per minute of the four-ton hammer"; whole apparatus
  seven tons) — the steam pile-driver, a different machine.
- **The 50-cwt hammers** of the Autobiography's order book (the
  Admiralty's 50-cwt Devonport order) — customers' machines.
- **The surviving full-size SMG hammer co46229** (Patricroft
  1845–55, Royal Mint until 1933) — SMG itself calls it "a small
  version of the giant machines Nasmyth built". A different
  machine; not measured for this build.
- **Later double-frame / "anvil-separate" production hammers** and
  the 1847 self-acting patent form (IMechE's 1:8 model is of that
  later works pattern) — not the 1851 exhibit's evidence.

machine's overall height H (floor at the feet → top of the lifting

comparison, so all figures are true fractions of H. Expect ±3% per
level, ±5% per width (near-elevation view, mild perspective).

## 8. Mechanism — as evidenced (for the build and NOTES)

From SMG's description of the pattern (co46218, mechanism evidence
only — see §3) + the exhibit photograph, which shows the same
fittings:

- **Single-acting, direct-action:** the hammer-head (tup) is
  connected to the piston of the vertical steam cylinder and is
  **guided between two standards**. Steam admitted **below** the
  piston lifts the tup; the valve then lets it **fall by gravity**
  onto the work. (Double-acting — steam above the piston — was a
  later improvement; SMG notes it as a subsequent development, and
  nothing in the 1851 evidence shows it. The build is
  single-acting.)
- **Slide valve** on the side/front of the cylinder, in the bolted
  valve chest visible in the photograph, worked by the **hand
  lever** (the long horizontal lever projecting left) — hand-gear
  is the original control.
- **Wilson's self-acting regulating device (1843):** the slide
  valve is also connected to the piston of a **small overhead
  cylinder**, with levers worked partly by a cam and partly by
  momentum, regulating the number and strength of blows
  irrespective of work thickness. The photograph shows the
  overhead gear, the vertical motion rod down the left side and
  the low handwheel; the build includes these in simplified form,
  flagged as pattern-evidence.
- **The one evidenced moving component (CP024):** the tup +
  piston rod as a single unit, travelling vertically between the
  guides. Modelled travel 1.30 m above the photographed rest
  position (bounded by the guide length and the cylinder
  underside clearance in the photograph's own proportions);
  the prop ships with the Tup at mid-stroke (+0.65 m).

## 9. Priority — the narrow claim only

Steam-hammer priority is **contested**: François Bourdon at Le
Creusot (Schneider) had a working hammer in 1840 from the same
design encounter (Nasmyth's own Autobiography tells the
Scheme-Book story against himself), and the Science Museum's text
for co46229 states plainly that Nasmyth "did not invent the steam
hammer, nor was he the first to build one." **The only claim this
prop makes: Nasmyth & Co exhibited a steam hammer at the Great
Exhibition in 1851** (Catalogue Class VI, No. 236; Council Medal).
No "inventor of the steam hammer" wording anywhere in the package.
